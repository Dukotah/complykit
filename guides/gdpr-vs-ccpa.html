GDPR vs CCPA: Key Differences and Which One Applies to You

The GDPR and CCPA are the two privacy laws most websites encounter first. They share similar goals — giving people control over their personal data — but they differ in who they cover, how consent works, and what you must put in your privacy policy. This guide maps the differences clearly so you can act on them.

Who each law covers

GDPR Scope

  • No size threshold. GDPR applies to any organization — startup, nonprofit, sole trader — that processes personal data of EU/EEA residents.
  • Established in EU — the processing must happen within the EU/EEA, OR
  • Targeting EU residents — you offer goods or services to EU residents (even for free), OR you monitor their behaviour (cookies, analytics, ad tracking).
  • US companies included. A US SaaS with no EU office but with European customers is subject to GDPR.
  • Processors covered too. Not just the company that owns the data (controller) — the cloud provider, analytics vendor, and email platform (processors) each have direct GDPR obligations.

CCPA/CPRA Scope

  • For-profit businesses only. Nonprofits and government agencies are exempt.
  • Must meet at least one threshold:
  • Annual gross revenue > $25 million, OR
  • Buy, sell, receive, or share the personal information of 100,000+ California consumers or households per year, OR
  • Derive 50%+ of annual revenue from selling or sharing California consumers' personal information.
  • Location-agnostic. A New York or London company that sells to Californians and meets a threshold must comply.
  • Service providers. Analogous to GDPR processors — service providers that receive data solely to perform services for the business have limited obligations.
Many small sites are GDPR-covered but CCPA-exempt. If your site uses Google Analytics or Meta Pixel and has any EU visitors, GDPR applies — regardless of revenue or size. CCPA only kicks in once a revenue or volume threshold is crossed.

Consent vs opt-out: the core philosophical difference

This is the biggest practical difference in day-to-day compliance work.

GDPR is opt-in. For non-essential processing — analytics, marketing, ad targeting — you must obtain consent before collecting or using data. That consent must be:

  • Freely given — not bundled with terms of service or made a condition of service
  • Specific — separate consent for each distinct purpose
  • Informed — users must know what they are consenting to before agreeing
  • Unambiguous — a pre-ticked checkbox does not count; silence is not consent

This is why GDPR-compliant cookie banners block non-essential cookies until the user clicks "Accept" — not "X" or "Continue."

CCPA/CPRA is opt-out. You may collect and use personal information by default. The obligation is to tell consumers you do so (via your privacy policy and a "Do Not Sell or Share" link) and to honor opt-out requests promptly. The exception is sensitive personal information under CPRA — consumers can direct businesses to limit the use and disclosure of sensitive data (health, precise geolocation, financial, biometric, and similar categories) even if they never opted in.

Practical impact: A US SaaS with EU users needs both a cookie consent banner (GDPR) and a "Do Not Sell or Share" link (CCPA if thresholds are met). These serve different users, so you generally need both mechanisms — though many CMPs (Consent Management Platforms) handle both in a single interface.

Which law applies to your business?

Work through these questions in order.

  1. Do you have any users, visitors, or customers in the EU/EEA? If yes — including if you use Google Analytics, Meta Pixel, or any third-party tracker that fires for EU visitors — GDPR applies. There is no size exemption; a one-person indie SaaS with 50 EU users must comply.
  2. Is your business for-profit? If no, you are likely outside CCPA scope. If yes, continue.
  3. Do you meet any of the CCPA/CPRA thresholds? Annual gross revenue >$25M; handle personal info of 100,000+ California consumers/households per year; or earn 50%+ of revenue from selling/sharing California personal info. If you meet any one of these, CCPA/CPRA applies.
  4. Do you have users in the UK? The UK retained the GDPR framework after Brexit as the UK GDPR — it is substantively identical to EU GDPR and is enforced by the UK Information Commissioner's Office (ICO). If you have UK users, treat them the same as EU users.
  5. Do you have users in other US states? Virginia (VCDPA), Colorado (CPA), Connecticut (CTDPA), Texas (TDPSA), and several others have passed GDPR-influenced privacy laws. The core obligations overlap significantly with CCPA/CPRA, so CCPA compliance gives you a reasonable head start on most of them.

Complying with both GDPR and CCPA at once

If both laws apply, the good news is that GDPR is the stricter framework — a site fully compliant with GDPR will satisfy most CCPA requirements automatically. The key areas where you need explicit CCPA work on top of GDPR:

  • "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link. CCPA requires this in the footer of your homepage and in your privacy policy if you sell or share personal information. GDPR does not have an equivalent link requirement.
  • Global Privacy Control (GPC). California requires you to treat the GPC browser signal as an opt-out of sale/sharing. GDPR has no GPC obligation, though honouring it aligns with GDPR's consent expectations.
  • Right not to be discriminated against. CCPA explicitly prohibits differential treatment of consumers who exercise their privacy rights. GDPR has no direct equivalent.
  • Sensitive personal information limits. CPRA's right to limit use of sensitive data has a different scope than GDPR's conditions for processing special-category data — review both and apply the stricter standard for each data type.

Your privacy policy needs to cover both frameworks. A single document with clearly labeled jurisdiction sections ("EU/EEA residents," "California residents") is the standard pattern. It satisfies both regulators and reduces reader confusion.

Frequently asked questions

Does GDPR apply to US companies?

Yes, if you offer goods or services to EU/EEA residents or monitor their behaviour (e.g. via analytics or cookies), GDPR applies to you regardless of where your company is based. A US SaaS with European customers must comply with GDPR even if it has no office in Europe.

Does CCPA apply to businesses outside California?

Yes. The CCPA (and CPRA) applies to any for-profit business — wherever it is located — that meets the thresholds and collects personal information from California residents. A New York or London company that sells to Californians and meets a threshold must comply.

What are the CCPA thresholds?

Under the CPRA (which amended CCPA in 2023), a business must comply if it: (1) has annual gross revenue over $25 million; (2) buys, sells, receives, or shares the personal information of 100,000 or more California consumers or households per year; or (3) derives 50% or more of its annual revenue from selling or sharing California consumers' personal information. Nonprofits are exempt.

What is the difference between GDPR consent and CCPA opt-out?

GDPR follows an opt-in model: for most non-essential processing — especially for marketing and analytics — you must obtain the user's freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent before collecting data. CCPA follows an opt-out model: you may collect and use data by default, but you must give California consumers the right to opt out of the sale or sharing of their personal information at any time.

What is the "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link?

CCPA/CPRA requires businesses that sell or share personal information to display a prominent "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link on their homepage and in their privacy policy. Clicking it must let California consumers opt out without creating an account or providing unnecessary personal information. The Global Privacy Control (GPC) signal must also be honoured automatically.

What are the GDPR fines vs CCPA fines?

GDPR fines can reach €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. The CCPA/CPRA imposes civil penalties of up to $7,500 per intentional violation and $2,500 per unintentional violation. Consumers also have a private right of action — $100–$750 per consumer per incident — for certain data breaches involving non-encrypted data.

Can one privacy policy cover both GDPR and CCPA?

Yes. Many businesses use a single privacy policy that includes jurisdiction-specific sections: a "Your GDPR rights (EU/EEA residents)" section and a "Your CCPA/CPRA rights (California residents)" section. The universal disclosures — what data you collect, why, and with whom you share it — satisfy both laws when written clearly. ComplyKit generates a layered privacy policy that handles both.
