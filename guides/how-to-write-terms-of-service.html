How to Write a Terms of Service for Your Website

A Terms of Service tells users what they can and cannot do on your site, limits your liability when things go wrong, and gives you a clear path to remove abusive accounts. Here is the eight-clause checklist that covers every site — from a solo blog to a funded SaaS product.

Why you need a Terms of Service

A privacy policy tells users what you do with their data. A Terms of Service tells users what they are allowed to do with your product — and what happens if they do not comply.

Without one, you have no contractual basis to remove a user who harasses others, no clear ownership claim over the platform while users submit content, and limited grounds to cap your liability if someone misuses your service and tries to hold you responsible. Courts in the US and EU consistently give more weight to a clearly written ToS than to silence.

A ToS also signals professionalism. Investors, enterprise buyers, and app stores all expect to find one. Omitting it raises more flags than including it.

The 8 essential clauses

Every ToS is different, but these eight clauses address the questions that come up for almost every website or web app.

  1. Acceptance of terms. State clearly how users agree to the ToS — by visiting the site, creating an account, or clicking an explicit "I agree" button. Clickwrap agreements (an explicit checkbox or button) are more enforceable than browsewrap (a footer notice alone). For anything involving payments or sensitive data, require an affirmative click.
  2. Permitted and prohibited uses. Describe what your service is for, then list what is not allowed: scraping, reverse engineering, impersonation, distributing malware, spamming, or using the service to harm others. Be specific. "Don't do anything illegal" is too vague to enforce; "do not use automated tools to extract data from the site" is not.
  3. Intellectual property and user content. Clarify who owns what. You almost certainly own the site's code, design, and original content. If users can submit content — posts, images, comments — you need a licence grant from them so you can display, store, and moderate it without infringing their copyright. A typical grant reads: "You grant us a non-exclusive, royalty-free licence to use your submissions to operate and improve the service."
  4. Accounts and authentication. If users create accounts, state that they are responsible for all activity under their login, must notify you of any unauthorised access, and must not share credentials. Include what happens to the account if the service terminates or the user violates the ToS.
  5. Disclaimers and limitation of liability. This is the most commercially important clause. State that the service is provided "as is" with no warranties, and cap your total liability to the amount the user paid you in the preceding 12 months (or a fixed figure). US courts generally enforce these limits; EU consumer law restricts them for B2C relationships, so flag this section for local legal review if you have significant European users.
  6. Indemnification. The user agrees to cover your legal costs if their misuse of the service causes a third party to sue you. For B2C products this clause may be limited or unenforceable in some jurisdictions, but it still discourages bad actors and establishes the expectation that misuse has consequences.
  7. Governing law and dispute resolution. Choose the jurisdiction whose courts will hear disputes and which law governs the agreement. For US businesses, a common choice is the state where you are incorporated. Consider including an informal good-faith resolution step before arbitration or litigation — many disputes settle at this stage, saving everyone time and money.
  8. Changes to the agreement. Explain how you will notify users of updates — by email, in-app banner, or a "last updated" date at the top of the document — and how much notice you will give. Platforms that change terms without notice face regulatory scrutiny and user backlash. Thirty days advance notice for material changes is a reasonable standard.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Copying another site's ToS verbatim. ToS documents are copyrighted. Copying one also means the clauses may not match your service, creating contradictions a court could use against you.
  • Using browsewrap for high-risk agreements. A footer link is not enough if your service involves payment, user-generated content, or significant liability risk. Require an affirmative click.
  • No "last updated" date. Without a date, you cannot demonstrate which version a user agreed to. Add it at the top of the document and update it every time you make material changes.
  • Contradicting your Privacy Policy. If your ToS says "we do not sell data" but your Privacy Policy says you share with ad partners, you have a compliance problem. Keep both documents in sync.
  • Forgetting jurisdiction-specific rules. GDPR restricts certain liability limitations for EU consumers. California's CCPA has its own disclosure requirements. If you have significant international traffic, flag those sections for local legal review.

Keeping your ToS current

A ToS is not a "write once and forget" document. Set a calendar reminder to review it annually and whenever you:

  • Launch a new feature that changes how users interact with the service
  • Start accepting payments or change your pricing model
  • Add integrations that share user data with third parties
  • Receive a cease-and-desist or legal notice relating to a clause
  • Expand to a new country with different consumer protection laws

Each update should increment the "last updated" date and, for material changes, trigger a notification to your user base before the changes take effect.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Terms of Service for a free website?

Yes. Even free websites benefit from a ToS because it establishes what users may and may not do with your content, limits your liability for how users misuse the site, and gives you grounds to ban abusive accounts or terminate access. The risks of not having one — especially around liability and intellectual property — exist regardless of whether you charge money.

Are ToS documents legally binding?

Yes, provided users have a genuine opportunity to read them and actively agree (clickwrap) or are given clear notice (browsewrap). Clickwrap agreements — requiring users to check a box or click "I Agree" — are more enforceable than a footer link alone. Courts in most jurisdictions will uphold a clearly displayed ToS with a reasonable acceptance mechanism.

What is the difference between Terms of Service and Terms of Use?

The names are used interchangeably. "Terms of Service" (ToS), "Terms of Use" (ToU), and "Terms and Conditions" (T&C) all refer to the same type of legal agreement that governs the relationship between a website operator and its users. Pick whichever label fits your brand and use it consistently.

Can I copy a Terms of Service from another website?

No. ToS documents are protected by copyright, and copying one verbatim can expose you to infringement claims. More practically, a ToS copied from a different business is unlikely to address your specific service, jurisdiction, or user base accurately. Use a generator or template and customise it for your situation.

How often should I update my Terms of Service?

Review your ToS whenever you add a new feature, change how you handle user data, modify your payment terms, or hear of a relevant court ruling in your jurisdiction. A yearly review is a reasonable minimum. Most agreements include a "we may update these terms" clause that requires you to notify users — commonly by email or a banner — before changes take effect.

Do I need a lawyer to write a Terms of Service?

For most small websites and apps, a well-structured template or generator provides a solid starting point. You should consult a lawyer if you handle sensitive user data, operate in a heavily regulated industry (finance, health, legal services), accept payments globally, or your business generates significant revenue. A lawyer can tailor indemnification clauses and governing law provisions to your specific risk profile.
